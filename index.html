Show the rate calculation method

Average rate = (final temperature − initial temperature) ÷ total time, measured in °C/min.

What might cause different groups to obtain different results?

Consider lamp distance or output, initial temperature, the position of the thermometer bulb, slight changes in timing, bottle properties and how consistently readings were taken. Different groups provide useful comparisons, but are not automatically three repeat trials under identical conditions.

What is the heat-transfer explanation?

Radiation travels from the lamp to the bottles. Dark surfaces generally absorb more of the lamp's visible radiation and lighter surfaces reflect more. The warmed bottle transfers energy to the air by contact, with convection distributing that energy. Real absorption also depends on the material and wavelengths emitted by the lamp.

Question 1 · 2 marks

Control a variable—and explain why

A fair test changes bottle colour and measures the rate of air-temperature change. Choose a control to connect the method to the physics.

Reveal a full 2-mark model answer

Keep every bottle 10 cm from an equivalent lamp. Changing the distance changes the radiant energy arriving per second, which could change the air’s heating rate independently of colour. Keeping distance constant makes the colour comparison fair.

Question 2 · 1 mark

“Empty” bottles contain the thing we are modelling

Bottle + air → Car + cabin air

The thermometer measures air inside a model car. The bottle wall represents the car’s outer surface.

Why use air instead of water?

Air models the air inside a car. Filling the same bottle with water would introduce a much larger thermal capacity and change its warming behaviour, so it would model a different system.

A bottle cannot reproduce car glazing, ventilation, shape or interior materials. Limit your conclusion to the model and conditions tested.

Step 1 · Calculate temperature change and rate

ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial
Average rate = ΔT ÷ elapsed time

Use the actual elapsed interval for an unfinished run. The handwritten rates in the source class sheet contain some arithmetic inconsistencies; this guide recalculates rates from the temperatures.

Step 2 · Use a best-fit line to support your conclusion

Choose two widely spaced points on the fitted line, read their coordinates, then calculate (T₂ − T₁) ÷ (t₂ − t₁). These need not be measured points. The fit-gradient column reports that slope; it may differ from the measured endpoint rate.

Step 3 · Check the graph against the mark scheme

Radiation carries energy from the lamp; conduction transfers it through the wall; convection redistributes it through the air.

The moving yellow markers represent energy, not pieces of matter. Fluid dots track small parcels of air, not individual molecules. Wall vibrations are enlarged to make them visible. Air next to the wall first gains energy by conduction.

4c · Why repeat? (1 mark)

Repeats test whether the results are repeatable and help assess reliability. Compare the spread and calculate a mean to reduce the influence of random variation. Repeats do not remove a systematic error.

The Part A method requests three trials. The supplied class sheet records one run per group: comparing groups provides evidence about reproducibility, but is not three controlled repeats by each group.

4d · Two method improvements (2 marks)

1. Check the radiant output at each bottle position using an appropriate radiation sensor, then equalise the lamps and distances. This reduces differences in energy input.

2. Run the heating for longer under the same controlled conditions and continue recording at equal intervals. This tests whether the colour ranking persists as heating slows.

Other extensions: test more colours, or repeat the entire colour comparison at several distances. At each distance, keep it the same for all colours; do not mix changed distances within one fair comparison.

Reveal variables and safety · 4 marks

Independent: bottle type/construction. Dependent: change in water temperature over time (or cooling rate). Control: same volume and starting temperature of water, room conditions and, where possible, bottle shape and lid arrangement.

Safety with a reason: use suitable heat-resistant gloves when handling hot bottles to reduce the risk of skin burns; wear eye protection to protect against splashes of hot water.

Reveal the evidence-based comparison · 2 marks

The insulated flask kept the water warmest: its temperature dropped only 4 °C in 60 minutes, compared with 16 °C for steel and 26 °C for plastic. Mean cooling-rate magnitudes were 0.067, 0.267 and 0.433 °C/min respectively. Signed temperature-change rates are negative.

For equal masses of water, Q = mcΔT means the larger temperature drop corresponds to a greater decrease in the water’s thermal energy. The flask limits transfer through its insulating construction.

This ranking applies to these tested bottles. Steel conducts heat well: these results do not show that steel itself is a better insulator than plastic. Wall thickness, lids and construction also affect cooling.

Question 6 · 3 marks

Why can a black shirt feel hotter in sunshine?

Black fabric generally absorbs more incoming sunlight, increasing the energy available to warm the fabric and wearer.

Reveal a complete explanation

The Sun emits electromagnetic radiation, which can travel through the vacuum of space and be absorbed by clothing or skin. Under otherwise similar conditions, black clothing generally absorbs more sunlight and white clothing reflects more, so a black shirt can become warmer.

Fabric, fit, airflow and sweating also matter. Visible colour alone does not determine how a material absorbs or emits every infrared wavelength.

Question 7 · 4 marks

A saucepan is a circulation system

Follow the ★ water parcel

1 · Warm and rise

Near the heated base, water expands. It becomes less dense than nearby water and rises.

Choose a step to freeze the starred parcel in that part of its journey.

Orange = warmer waterBlue = cooler water

Dots are tracked parcels of water, not bubbles. Their colour changes as they warm and cool. Real flow is less regular than these simplified convection loops.

Step 1 · Which way do the arrows go? (1 mark)

Show water rising directly above the heat source, moving outward near the surface, sinking at the cooler sides, then returning along the bottom. These closed paths are convection cells.

Step 2 · Why does the water move? (3 marks)

Water heated at the bottom expands and becomes less dense than the surrounding water, so buoyancy causes it to rise. Cooler water is denser and sinks, setting up circulation that transfers thermal energy.

The supplied mark scheme contains a typo saying cooler water becomes “less dense and drops”; the correct statement is more dense and sinks. Convection can begin before boiling. Steam bubbles are a separate process and are not the convection current itself.
